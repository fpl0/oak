<Step id="S1">
The major V of Raise the Seventh can also be reached by walking down to it. Four chords on an acoustic guitar, falling a step at a time: the descent behind flamenco, surf guitar and Hit the Road Jack.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>The bass walks down one step a bar, and the last chord sounds unfinished, leaning forward. So each round ends wanting the next one.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Put the last bar next to the first before you play it again.

</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Now the names: four numerals, and one note that changes on the way down.

<Example id="the-descent" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>i VII VI V, Am G F E. The roots walk down from home a step at a time, and the walk ends on the major V you made in the last lesson. VII keeps G natural; only V raises it to G♯. This descent is the **Andalusian cadence**: both minors in one loop, which is why it is written in chord names.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
This version of the walk has lost its lean.

<Editor exercise="land-on-the-v" />

<OnPass>Am G F E: the descent now leans on G♯ and falls back into A each time round. You have the whole gesture in your hands.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Every loop you have learned since The Four-Chord Schema, mixed together: four that live in major, two in minor.

<Choice exercise="name-the-minor-loop" />

<OnPass>The Aeolian loop circles; the Andalusian cadence walks down and leans home. Both join the loops in your warm-up.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
The descent lives in its bass. Put it in your voice.

<Sing exercise="sing-the-walk-down" />

<OnPass>Do te le sol. The last step, le to sol, is the half step that makes the walk sound like it is arriving somewhere. Falling from do to sol like this, the line has carried laments for centuries.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
The starter plays Am over every note of the bass. This time you build the chords from the bass up, the way Hear the Bass First taught you to listen. Its pace and colour are yours: slow and wide, or two chords a bar as in Harmonic Rhythm. Your loop opens your song: its first four bars come before the first verse, where Oak walks down Am G F E today, so the verse comes in on Am right after the E.
