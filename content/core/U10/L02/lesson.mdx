<Step id="S1">
The Dark Twin kept C as home and lowered three notes. That is one way to stand a minor key next to a major one, and this groove visits a second.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>C major and C minor both stand on C: they share a home. A minor stands on A, a new home, and is just as dark. So C major has two minor neighbours, and the next steps find out what the second one keeps.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Now home is A. Picture the keyboard from A up to the next A.

<Predict exercise="how-many-black-keys" />

<OnPass>None. A minor plays the white keys from A to A: the same seven notes as C major, with a new home.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
All three scales side by side, so you can see what each minor keeps from C major.

<Example id="three-scales" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>A minor uses exactly C major's notes, with home on degree 6 of C. It is C major's **relative minor**. C minor keeps C as home and lowers 3, 6 and 7, which takes three flats. It is C major's **parallel minor**. Every major key has both twins, one with the same notes and one with the same home.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
The dark loop of The Dark Twin was the parallel twin before it had a name. Here is a new loop in C major to move the same way.

<Editor exercise="the-twin-at-home" />

<OnPass>Three words changed, `major` to `minor`, and home stayed on C. The numbers in `n()` never moved: in degrees, the parallel twin is one word away.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Any major key's relative minor starts on its degree 6. Count up the scale; no sound needed.

<Choice exercise="find-the-relative" />

<OnPass>D minor shares F major's notes, E minor shares G major's. On the circle of fifths each major key has its relative minor beside it, with the same sharps or flats.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Twins with the same notes share a key signature too.

<StaffInput exercise="d-minor-signature" />

<OnPass>One flat, B♭: the signature of F major, D minor's relative. A minor has none, like C major. The minor signatures join your key-signature cards.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
This is the four-chord loop of The Four-Chord Schema, in C major, with a tune that ends on C. The twin with the same notes moves home to A: the same numbers, counted from A, turn the loop dark. In A minor it plays the first verse of your song, the eight bars after the opening, where Oak plays the same chords, Am Em F Dm, under a tune that also comes home on A.

<Make exercise="home-on-a" />

<Callout kind="tip">Let the loop travel: four bars in C major, four in A minor, with a key plan in each `.scale()` such as `<C4:major!4 A3:minor!4>`, the pattern you met in Same Song, Any Key. The same notes change mood when home moves, a move many pop songs make between verse and chorus.</Callout>
