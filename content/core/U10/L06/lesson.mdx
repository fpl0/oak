<Step id="S1">
In major, V to I is the strongest way home: ti leans into do and the phrase closes. The minor loops you have written so far circle instead. Here is a slow minor loop, twice, with one chord changed the second time.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>The second version arrives: bar 4 lands the way a cadence lands in major. The first comes home too, but drifts in without a push. The difference starts in bar 3.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Read the chord names in bar 3 of each loop.

<Predict exercise="em-becomes-e" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
That one note belongs to a scale. Hear natural minor, then the scale with the change, then one chord the new scale makes.

<Example id="two-minors" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>Natural minor with degree 7 raised a half step, G to G♯, is **harmonic minor**. Its 7 is ti again, a half step below do, and it turns the chord on 5 into E major. Bar 3 shows a side effect: stacked on ♭3, the same scale gives C E G♯, an augmented chord. That is why this lesson writes its chords by name.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
Now put the raised 7 where it does its work: in the chord on 5. The chord band reads each chord as a numeral while you edit.

<Editor exercise="raise-it" />

<OnPass>Am Dm E Am: i iv V i. G♯ in the chord and in the tune leans into A, and the loop lands each time round. When V leads home in a minor key, classical music almost always raises the 7, for exactly this pull.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Now without the climbing tune to help you.

<Choice exercise="v-or-big-v" />

<OnPass>The minor v drifts home; the major V pulls. This choice comes back among the numerals in your warm-up.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Sol, then ti, then do: the leading tone you sang in major, now in a minor key.

<Sing exercise="sol-ti-do" />

<OnPass>Sol ti do over V and i. In minor, ti exists only because the 7 was raised; without it the last step would be te to do, a whole step.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
Your Aeolian loop from the last lesson is back in the editor. It circles without arriving, which suits the first two choruses of your song; the last chorus, after the bridge, is where it lands, and today's loop plays there. Oak's last chorus already lands: Am F C G, then Am F E Am, its tune climbing G♯ to A. Where yours lands is your choice, and a tune shaped around the landing makes it the moment you wait for each time round.

<Make exercise="a-loop-that-lands" />

<CanDo id="cd.compose.harmonic-minor" />

Until today your minor loops could only circle. Now you can make one land, and choose where. A loop that circles, then a V that lands it: that is the verse and chorus of the minor ballad you will write after the Minor checkpoint. Next: the descent that walks down to the same V.
