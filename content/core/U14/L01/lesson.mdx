<Step id="S1">
Until now every key you have written in has been one of two things, major or minor. This lesson changes one note of a scale you know and gets a third colour. Hear it before it has a name.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>Same numbers, same rhythm, yet the second half sounds looser and earthier, closer to a rock riff than to a bright major tune. Something in the scale under the numbers changed.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
The numbers in `n` stayed put, so the scale under them must have changed.

<Predict exercise="how-many-notes-move" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
The same eight numbers on both scales, from home up to home.

<Example id="two-scales" views="code,roll,staff" />

<OnPass>One note: degree 7. G major has F♯, a half step under home; this scale has F, a whole step under it. On the staff that F needs a natural sign, the one accidental on the page. The scale is **Mixolydian**: major with a ♭7, the te you sang in minor and in the blues, now inside a major scale. Major, minor and Mixolydian are the same kind of thing, a set of notes around one home with its own colour. Such a scale is called a **mode**.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
Now build it yourself, by degree.

<OnPass>1 2 3 4 5 6 ♭7: six notes shared with G major and one that is not. That one note is the **characteristic tone** of the scale, the note that tells your ear which colour it is hearing. For Mixolydian it is ♭7. With F a whole step under home, nothing in the scale strains up into G any more, and that is the looser sound you heard in the riff.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Back to the riff, and this time you choose its scale.

<Editor exercise="one-word-twice" />

<OnPass>One word in each line, and the F♯s became Fs. The numbers never changed: `n` 6 is still degree 7, and the scale decides which 7 that is.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
The colour is not tied to G.

<Choice exercise="major-or-mixolydian" />

<OnPass>A half step under home pulls: major. A whole step under home leans back: Mixolydian. You will hear that difference in a lot of rock and folk from now on.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
Your riff and its bass play the first verse of your song, the eight bars after the opening, over Oak's drums and nothing else. Once your riff plays its ♭7, take it further with the last unit's tools: push a note ahead of the bar line with `.early()`, or let one ring across a beat.

<Make exercise="your-mixolydian-riff" />

You can now give a major scale a third colour with one note, and hear it. Next: the checkpoint on sevenths and rhythm; then the chord that F brings with it, and the three-chord rock loop it makes.

</Step>
