<Step id="S1">
Your soul loop jumped between chords of the key. These chords walk instead: one degree a bar, up from Cmaj7 and back down, a common move in soul and R&B.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>Bright, softer, softer, bright: Cmaj7, two minor seventh chords, then Fmaj7. Every note came from C major, so the key itself decided which step got which kind of seventh.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
One stack, moved up the key a degree a bar, from C to B. No chord is named in the code.

<Example id="seven-stacks" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>Imaj7, ii7, iii7, IVmaj7, V7, vi7, viiø7. Major sevenths on 1 and 4, minor sevenths on 2, 3 and 6, the half-diminished on 7, and one dominant seventh, only on 5. So inside a major key a dominant seventh points home, a 4th above its root. The blues bends that rule on purpose, with a dominant seventh on every chord, home included.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
The case of a numeral still comes from its triad; the ending now comes from its seventh.

<OnPass>Imaj7, vi7, ii7, V7: home, the minor chord on 6, the chord before V, and V7 leaning back home. You can read any loop in a key this way now.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
No code for the tune and no roll: only the page, its first chord, and four numerals. The key signature tells you where degree 1 is.

<Editor exercise="play-from-the-page" />

<OnPass>Gmaj7, Am7, Bm7, Cmaj7: the staircase of C, moved to G. The numerals did not change, only the letters, and the tune's F♯ now sits inside Bm7.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">

<Step id="S7">

The key's seven chords can turn it into the opening and the outro of your song, where Oak climbs Dm7 Em7 Fmaj7 G7, and a G7 in the last bar leans into the first verse and, at the end, into one last Cmaj7.
