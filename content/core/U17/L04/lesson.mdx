<Step id="S1">
The gym has given you tunes, basses and changes to take down for months, each in one key. Today's songs move home halfway through. First, just listen, with nothing on the screen.

<OnPass>Somewhere in the middle your do stopped fitting. The tune began again, and the note you were humming was no longer home.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Before any code, the two things a transcriber needs: where home moves, and how far. You found the bar in Hearing Key Changes and compared the two lifts in Truck-Driver & Direct Modulation.

<Choice exercise="where-and-how-far" />

<OnPass>Bar 5, a whole step: the chorus starts again from B instead of A, over G instead of F. Everything after that bar is the same song in a new key. That is all you need to write it down.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Take the tune down as you always have, by degrees. Switch between the source and your code at the same bar to check. Four bars of numbers are enough: the key plan writes the rest.

<Editor exercise="take-the-tune-down" />

<OnPass>Four bars of numbers and `<F4:major!4 G4:major!4>`. You heard the same tune twice, so you wrote it once and told the code where home moves.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
A different kind of key change now. Jazz ballads of the 1920s and 30s often move home at the start of their middle section, sometimes by the smallest step there is. This one is Oak's, in that shape.

<OnPass>Home moved at bar 3, and the tenor sang its opening phrase again on the new footing. A middle section in another key is a common way for a song to sound fresh without a new tune.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Five flats will not get in your way: the bass is written in degrees, and the key is one string. Write bars 1 and 2 first, then find the new do.

<Editor exercise="the-bass-through-the-door" />

<OnPass>D♭ major for two bars, then D major for four: a half step. The roots sing 2 5, 1 on both sides of the change, then 6 2, 5 in the new key. The chords of bar 3, Em7 and A7, are the new key's own ii and V7: they announce the new home a bar before it arrives.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Back to the pop chorus. Its chords and its tune are there; the bass is yours. In your song these eight bars are the lift before the last chorus: F major, then G major, and the last chorus picks up in D. Everything from Bass Lines counts here, from Root–Five–Octave to the approach notes of Walking Up to It.

<Make exercise="your-bass-both-keys" />

<CanDo id="cd.transcribe.key-change" />

You can now take a tune down through its key change: find the bar, find the new do, and let a key plan carry the degrees across. That is the last new idea of the core course. It began with a drum loop and a pulse; it ends with a bass line that follows a song into a new key. Next: the checkpoint on colour chords and key changes, and then Chromatic Colour, a song of yours that uses all of them.
