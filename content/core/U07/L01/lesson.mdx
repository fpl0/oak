<Step id="S1">
You can write a tune in degrees and set chords under it. This one runs eight bars over the band's eight-bar phrase, and the roll draws it as a line.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>Mostly small moves: the next degree up or down, now and then a skip of a 3rd, and just one big jump. Small moves join the notes into one line a voice can sing, so the single big jump stands out.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
The numbers draw the same picture before a note sounds.

<Predict exercise="read-the-shape" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Back to the eight-bar tune. The roll puts every note at its height, so you can measure the line.

<Example id="find-the-top" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>The tune climbs to A in bar 2, dips back to D in bar 4 and climbs again. On beat 1 of bar 6 it leaps a 6th to F5, the only time it goes that high, and from there it walks home. The up-and-down shape of a melody is its **contour**.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
Every note in this line belongs to its chord, and it still sounds like a box of parts. The problem is its contour: it jumps from low to high and back with nothing between.

<Editor exercise="tame-the-line" />

<OnPass>Now the line climbs from home to degree 6 (A) by step and turns back. Inside an octave and a 3rd, a voice can sing all of it.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
A tune spends its high note once. Where it spends it changes what the whole tune feels like.

<Choice exercise="where-is-the-top" />

<OnPass>With its high note in bar 6 the tune keeps building for most of its length, then lets go. That high point, reached once and on purpose, is the tune's **climax**. Many tunes save it for the second half.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
No roll this time. Hum along if it helps you feel which way each tune goes.

<Choice exercise="hear-the-shape" />

<OnPass>Climb, fall, arch, valley: most tunes are made of these four, one phrase at a time.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
On the staff the contour is the same picture: the notes rise up the page to the F in bar 6 and come back down to home.

<Staff example="on-the-page" />
</Step>

<Step id="S9">
Look again at bar 6 of the hook: a leap up to F, then a walk back down. Here the leaps keep climbing instead.

<Editor exercise="leap-then-turn-back" />

<OnPass>Each leap now turns back toward the notes it jumped over, so the line sounds like one voice catching its breath. A leap followed by a turn back, often by a step, is a **gap-fill**. It is a habit of good tunes, not a law: a run of chord notes, C E G C, can climb on.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S8">
Your tune plays the second part of your song, the eight bars after the opening, over the band's eight-bar phrase. Oak's tune there climbs to one high note in bar 6 and comes home; the draft in the editor plays each chord as an arpeggio, so every move is a leap. A tune used to be right notes in a row; now you also decide where it goes.
