<Step id="S1">
In Call & Response one part answered another. Blues players talk the same way: one plays a short riff and leaves room, another fills the room. W. C. Handy's St. Louis Blues (1914) is built like this: each line is sung, then the band answers.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>Alone, the riff leaves a hole after every call, and the twelve bars feel half empty. With the organ answering, each line becomes a short exchange, and the space turns into part of the music.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Read the organ's line before you hear it.

</Step>

<Step id="S3">
The chords move under the riff. Does the riff move with them?

<Example id="riff-stays" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>The riff never changes, while the chords go from C7 to F7 to G7 under it: that is how a blues riff works. It comes round three times, once a line, and each time the organ answers.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
The guitar calls; the organ is waiting. Its answer starts higher, takes the call's notes somewhere else, and comes home.

<Editor exercise="answer-the-call" />

<OnPass>Call, answer, three times over the twelve bars: a whole chorus of blues conversation from one riff and one reply.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
In a sung blues the singer takes the first part of each line and the band answers in the rest. Hear where one hands over to the other.

<Choice exercise="where-the-answer-starts" />

<OnPass>The tune stops halfway through bar 3, and the organ answers from there to the end of the line. Singers leave that room on purpose: the answer is part of the song, as it was in the work songs and church singing the blues grew from.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Singing a riff back is how players learn them, and singing an answer is how they find their own.

<Sing exercise="sing-the-call-back" />

<OnPass>Do, me, fa, sol: the riff's first bar, with the blue third in it.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
The organ already answers, but the guitar never says the same thing twice, so nothing calls. Your blues plays the last chorus of your song, where Oak's version has one riff calling in every line and a new answer each time. A riff that comes back works as a motif, as in Motif & Variation, and both parts already swing, as in Shuffle.
