<Step id="S1">
The twelve bars have a fixed order, but players bend it in two places. Hear the blues you wrote last lesson, then a version many records use.

<OnPass>The second blues leaves home almost at once and settles straight back. Its last two bars move twice as fast, tipping it into the next time round. Same twelve bars, more motion.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Now find both changes on the chord band.

<Example id="where-it-moves" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>IV arrives in bar 2 and is gone by bar 3: the blues takes a quick look at IV, then settles home for the rest of the line. That is the **quick change**. The last two bars changed too, four chords in two bars, and you will write those as well.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
One edit in the first line.

<Editor exercise="iv-in-bar-two" />

<OnPass>C7, F7, C7, C7: the quick change. The first line moves now, and the rest of the form is the one you know.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
The end of the form decides how the next chorus begins. Two chords a bar make the last two bars push forward.

<Editor exercise="back-to-the-top" />

<OnPass>I–IV, then I–V: bar 12 ends on G7, and G7 pulls straight back to C7 at the top. Those last bars, written to lead back round, are the **turnaround**. Every chorus now ends by asking for another.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Players hear a quick change from the first two bars. So can you.

<EarDrill family="fam.frm.blues" level={3} trials={6} />

<OnPass>Bar 2 tells you: if the chord moves and comes straight back, it is a quick change.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
Your blues moves to G, a key that sits well on the guitar. The numerals stay and the names change, as when you moved a tune in Same Song, Any Key. In G the blue 3rd is B♭.

<Make exercise="your-blues-round-and-round" />

<CanDo id="cd.write.turnaround" />

You can now write a blues in any key from its numerals, with the two moves players add to it. Next: the same twelve bars, swung, so the blues stops marching and starts to lope.

</Step>
