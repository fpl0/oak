<Step id="S1">
A whole unit of minor lowered and raised notes of the major scale. This lesson takes two notes away instead. Back to the four-chord loop, with two tunes in the same rhythm.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>The first tune grinds against the chords at a few moments; the second, in the same rhythm, never does. The difference is not the rhythm but the notes each tune is allowed to use.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
The second tune's scale has a longer name than any you have used, and its name says what it keeps.

<Predict exercise="which-five" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
The second tune once more, with every note labelled.

<Example id="five-notes" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>Degrees 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6: the major scale without 4 and 7, the two notes that sit a half step from a neighbour. A five-note scale like this is a **pentatonic scale**, and this one is the major pentatonic. With the half steps gone, the worst any note does over the loop is add a colour, like E over F; nothing grinds the way F did over C in the first tune. In code `n` counts the five notes, so `n` 3 is degree 5 and `n` 4 is degree 6.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
Counting on five has a cost: a line written for seven notes changes when its scale does.

<Editor exercise="same-notes-new-numbers" />

<OnPass>C D E G, A G E, now as `n` 0 1 2 3 and 4 3 2: the same notes, counted on five.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Seven notes and five sound different, even as a quick run.

<Choice exercise="five-or-seven" />

<OnPass>The major scale walks in small steps, two of them half steps. The pentatonic leaps over 4 and 7, so it has two wider gaps, between 3 and 5 and between 6 and the top 1. That open sound is the one you hear in folk tunes, soul hooks and guitar solos.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
The same five degrees work from any home, G included.

<OnPass>G A B D E: degrees 1 2 3 5 and 6 of G major, with C (degree 4) and F♯ (degree 7) left out.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
Five notes, and no wrong ones over this loop: any number you pick will fit. Changing one number at a time and keeping what sounds good is improvising. A figure you like can come back later in the tune, as in Motif & Variation.

<Make exercise="five-notes-any-order" />

<CanDo id="cd.compose.pentatonic" />

Until now any note you chose could clash. On these five, you can play freely over a loop and keep what sounds good. Next comes the Minor checkpoint, which opens your minor indie ballad. After it, the same five notes start from 6: the minor pentatonic, and your first riff.

<Recap />
</Step>
