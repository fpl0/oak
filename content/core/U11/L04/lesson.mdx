<Step id="S1">
Blue notes rubbed against the chords from outside. Here one moves inside the chord: C and F as plain triads, then the same two chords with one note added to each.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>With the added note the chords stop sitting still. Each one leans, as if it wants to go somewhere, and the vamp starts to sound like the blues. The next step works out which note does it.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
In a chord symbol a 7 after the letter adds one note to the chord.

<Predict exercise="what-the-seven-adds" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Four bars: the vamp's two chords, then G7 and a plain C. Hear G7 lean and C settle.

<Example id="the-pull" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>C, E, G and B♭: the C major triad with a ♭7 on top, ten half steps above the root. That is a **dominant seventh**, named after the chord on 5, the dominant, where a major key builds one from its own notes. It sounds restless, as if it wants to move: G7 leans into C, and in the blues every chord leans like that.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
The same recipe works from any root, F included.

<OnPass>F, A, C and E♭: F7. The ♭7 is a whole step below the octave, on a black key here.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Back in code, with a whole loop to change at once.

<Editor exercise="seventh-every-chord" />

<OnPass>C7, F7, G7, C7. One character a chord, and the polite loop now sounds like the blues.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
In code the 7 is in the name. By ear, you have only the sound.

<Choice exercise="triad-or-seventh" />

<OnPass>Settled or leaning: that difference is the ♭7, and you can hear it now in any key and any voicing.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
The vamp is back in plain triads, with the hook's riff on top. It plays the four bars just before your song's first chorus, where Oak's version already leans on C7 and F7. The bend into the 3rd from Blue Notes fits this vamp too: E♭ rising to E over C7 is the blues in two notes.
