<Step id="S1">
Sharp Keys gathered sharps, one key at a time. Today the other half of the keys gathers flats.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>A small shift, every time. Each new home is the 4 of the old one, and keys that close share almost every note.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
You met F major once already, in Home & the Major Scale. Build it again from the pattern.

<Predict exercise="which-note-is-flat" />

<OnPass>B♭. From A, a half step up lands on B♭; B natural would be a whole step. F major has one flat.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Picture the keys as a clock face with C at twelve. Clockwise, each step goes to the key whose home is degree 5 of the last and adds a sharp: G, D, A, E, B, F♯. Anticlockwise, each home is degree 4 of the last and each step adds a flat.

<Example id="clockwise" views="code,roll" />

Neighbours on the clock share all but one note of their scales: C major and G major differ only in F and F♯. Keys across the clock share only two, and you can hear the difference.

<OnPass>F is one step anticlockwise with one flat, then B♭ with two, E♭ with three. Twelve places fit round the clock, each a sharp or a flat from its neighbours: the **circle of fifths**, named for that degree 5 from one home to the next. Three places at the bottom have two names, such as F♯ or G♭. They are the same keys on the piano, spelled with sharps or with flats, so there are fifteen major keys in all.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
Flats keep their own order, **B E A D G C F**: the order of sharps, backwards. Each flat key keeps the flats of the one before and adds the next.

<StaffInput exercise="b-flat-major-flats" />

<OnPass>B♭, then E♭. The last flat is always degree 4 of the key: E♭ in B♭ major, A♭ in E♭ major.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Now spell a flat key in code, letter by letter. The staff shows each note as you write it.

<Editor exercise="spell-e-flat-major" />

<OnPass>`eb4 f4 g4 ab4 bb4 c5 d5 eb5`: three flats, B♭ E♭ A♭, the first three of the order. Every letter once.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Round the circle without a picture: one step clockwise, or one step back.

<Choice exercise="one-step-round" />

<OnPass>One step round the circle, either way, is one sharp or one flat away. When a classical piece in a major key moves to another key, it most often goes one step clockwise, to the key on its degree 5, before it comes home.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
With the circle you can find the sharps or flats of any major key without spelling its scale. Your line plays the quiet eight bars of your song in F, over F and B♭ chords; in note names, you supply the flat. A held `f` at the end, with `@`, gives home time to land.

<Make exercise="every-b-flat" />

<Callout kind="tip">Try the near and the far key with a whole tune. Give `n("<[0 2 4 2] [3 1 0 ~]>")` from Same Song, Any Key the plan `<C4:major!2 G4:major!2>`, then `<C4:major!2 F#4:major!2>`. One step round changes a single note of the scale; half-way round, five of the seven change.</Callout>
