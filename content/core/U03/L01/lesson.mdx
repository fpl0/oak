<Step id="S1">
Ode to Joy's first four bars stopped on `d4` and sounded unfinished. Most tunes lean on one note like that, even when nobody names it. This line goes up, turns, and comes down to rest.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>The line comes to rest on `c4`, and a held `c` fits under both of the band's chords. Every other note of the line seems to circle that one.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
No band now, so each ending stands on its own. Two climbs up the white keys, and one stops a single note sooner.

<Choice exercise="which-climb-arrives" />

<OnPass>A arrives; B leaves you hanging on `b4`, waiting for the `c5` it never plays. The note a tune feels finished on is its **tonic**: home. Here home is C.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
From home to the next home, a climb takes eight notes and seven steps. Some of those steps are half steps, and where they fall matters.

<Example id="c-climb" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>Whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half: two half steps, at `e4`–`f4` and `b4`–`c5`, and whole steps everywhere else. That pattern from home to home is the **major scale**, and this one is C major.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
The pattern works from any note: whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half. The white keys give it to you only from C. From any other note, some steps come out wrong, and a sharp or a flat fixes them.

One more rule: each letter once. From F, the fourth note is a half step above `a4`. That key has two names, `a#4` and `bb4`. Write `bb4`: the scale already has an A, and the next letter after A is B.

<Example id="f-climb" views="code,roll" />

Seven letters, each used once, then home again an octave up. That is how every major scale is spelled.
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Now build one from G. The white keys from `g4` follow the pattern until near the top, where one step comes out wrong.

<Editor exercise="build-g-major" />

<OnPass>`f#5`: now the half step sits between the last two notes, where the pattern puts it, and every letter from G to F appears once. That one sharp gives G major the same lean into home that `b4` gave C.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Home by ear now, with no letters and no roll to lean on.

<EarDrill family="fam.deg.tonic" level={1} trials={5} />

<Callout kind="tip">After the phrase ends, keep humming. The note your voice settles on is usually home.</Callout>

<OnPass>Home, found by ear in several keys. It is not always the lowest note or the first one; it is the note the others lean towards.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
No keyboard this time: the pattern and the letters are enough.

<Choice exercise="spell-d-major" />

<OnPass>D E F# G A B C#, then D again: two sharps, each one fixing a step that was too short. Every major scale comes out the same way, with its own sharps or flats.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S8">
Last unit you learned to name any note. Now you know which one the music wants to end on. The band plays in C, so every note of C major fits over it, and `c` is home.

</Step>
