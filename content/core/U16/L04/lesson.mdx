<Step id="S1">
So far the borrowing has gone one way: a major key took darker chords from its minor. Today a minor song borrows the other way.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>The second verse ended on A major. After eight bars in the shade, the last chord opened into light, and the brighter D of bars 2 and 6 had already hinted at it.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
The two last chords are `Am` and `A`: one letter apart.

<Predict exercise="a-minor-becomes-a" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Both changes on their own, stacked by hand.

<Example id="two-pairs" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>A C E became A C♯ E, and D F A became D F♯ A: one note up a half step each time. The minor key on A borrows from the major key on A, the trade of the sad IV run the other way. Ending a minor piece on the major home chord is a **Picardy third**, after the raised 3rd that makes it. Composers of Bach's time ended many minor pieces this way. The bright IV is the chord you met in Dorian ♮6.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
Now both changes in E minor, in code.

<Editor exercise="brighten-two-chords" />

<OnPass>Em, A, B, E: i, IV, V, I. Two 3rds rose, G to G♯ in the last chord and C to C♯ in the second, and the phrase now ends in the light.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
In a minor key the numerals count against the minor scale, as they did in the minor unit. A borrowed major chord keeps its number and changes its case.

<OnPass>i IV V I. Degree 4 and degree 1 each appear in upper case, where D minor's own chords are lower case: both are borrowed from D major.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
The two endings differ by one note you can sing.

<Sing exercise="me-then-mi" />

<OnPass>Me, then mi: ♭3, then 3, one half step apart over the same home note. That half step is the whole of the Picardy third.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
Your verse ends in the shade. Its E chord already holds one bright note, G♯, the raised 7 of Raise the Seventh. One more raised note can make the ending bright.

<Make exercise="end-in-sunlight" />

When the loop comes round after the major chord, the verse begins again in A minor: the sun comes out, and the next verse starts in the shade.

Borrowing now runs both ways: darker chords into a major song, brighter ones into a minor song. Next: a lab, new chords under a tune you already know, with a tenor sax to play it.
