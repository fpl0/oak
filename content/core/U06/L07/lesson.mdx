<Step id="S1">
The four-chord loop is not the only one the records run on. Here are two more: a slow ballad with its chords broken into notes, then a fast loop that a band with three chords can play all night.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>The ballad turns minor in its second bar; the rock loop stays bright all the way round. Both are built from chords you can already name, in orders that songs have shared for decades.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Doo-wop and the four-chord loop use the same four chords. Only the order differs, and the order is what you hear.

<Choice exercise="which-loop-is-it" />

<OnPass>Doo-wop turns from I to the minor vi straight away, then moves through IV and V back home. The four-chord loop goes to V first.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Rock often needs fewer chords than pop. Count them.

<Example id="rock-loop" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>Three chords: I, IV and V, the three major triads of the key. Many rock and roll songs need nothing more, which is why this loop is called three-chord rock.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
Four loops now: the four-chord loop and its rotation from vi, doo-wop, and three-chord rock. Each comes in a new key, so listen for the moves, not the notes.

<EarDrill family="fam.har.schema" level={2} trials={6} />

<OnPass>Four loops that countless songs share, told apart by ear, in any key.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
In Endings, V went home. There is another way home: from IV.

<Editor exercise="the-amen-ending" />

<OnPass>IV, then I. It closes, but gently, without the pull of V: the "amen" sung at the end of a hymn. IV going to I at the end of a phrase is a **plagal cadence**. Rock and gospel songs end on it often.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Five endings now: closed and less closed from V, open on V, the swerve to vi, and the amen from IV.

<EarDrill family="fam.har.cadence" level={3} trials={6} />

<OnPass>The last two chords tell you the ending: what the last one is, and what came just before it.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
The starter is your four-chord loop in chord names; reorder its chords and it becomes doo-wop, the same four chords from a different decade. In your song, Oak plays doo-wop from I in the four bars before the last loud part, with three pulses to every beat; your loop takes those bars over. `.arp()`, as in Arpeggios, can break the chords three to a beat like the hook or in a rhythm of your own, and the loop can come home on the amen or on V to I.
