<Step id="S1">
Until now every chord loop moved once a bar. Here are the same drums, the same tempo and chords you know: listen to what happens halfway through.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>The second half hurries, though the drums never speed up. Something other than the tempo is setting the pace.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
In Two per Beat you squeezed two hits into one beat with `[ ]`. Brackets fit two roots into one bar the same way. Here the roots come first and the stack is added to each one, the other way round from The Seven Chords.

<Predict exercise="two-in-a-bar" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
The second half seemed to run, but the drums never sped up. Find what changed.

<Example id="hook-again" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>Two chords a bar in bars 5 to 7: the beat stays put while the chords come twice as often. How often the chords change is the song's **harmonic rhythm**. One chord a bar feels relaxed; two a bar pushes forward.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
A loop can save its faster changes for the end, so it hurries into the return of bar 1.

<Editor exercise="speed-up-the-ending" />

<OnPass>I, V, then vi IV and ii V, half a bar each. The last two bars hurry, and bar 1 arrives like a landing. Keep the roots first whenever a bar holds two chords: `.add()` keeps the rhythm of the pattern before it, so with the stack first each bar would play one chord.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
The same chords can sit back or push forward. Only their pace differs.

<Choice exercise="which-one-drives" />

<OnPass>Card B doubles its changes in bars 7 and 8 and lands home inside bar 8. Faster changes before the end make an ending feel driven.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Now by ear. The bass helps: it moves when the chord does.

<OnPass>Bars 2 and 4: IV V, then ii V. Bars 1 and 3 hold one chord each.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S8">
The chords' pace is not the tune's. Here the tune runs in eighth notes all the way, over two chords a bar; the last four bars are the breakdown, where the song pulls back.

<Editor exercise="busy-notes-slow-chords" />

<OnPass>The tune keeps running while the chords change half as often under it. The music stays busy and the ground beneath it slows down, so the breakdown breathes without stopping. A busy part over slow chords is one of the commonest pairings in pop and dance music.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
A bar used to hold one chord; now you decide how many, and where the loop hurries. Oak's climb into the loud parts hurries in its last bar, IV then V. Yours can hurry into one of the endings you know, V to I or the amen, with a bass on the roots moving at every change.
