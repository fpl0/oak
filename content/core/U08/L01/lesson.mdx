<Step id="S3">
On the roll the bass sits under the chords' stacks. Find where it moves and where it lands.

<Example id="on-the-one" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>The bass moves with each chord, on beat 1, and plays the note the chord is stacked on: its root. Under Am that is A, degree 6. The chord now stands on its own note.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
A new loop. The chords are written; the bass is yours.

<Editor exercise="roots-for-a-new-loop" />

<OnPass>Do, la, fa, sol: degrees 1 6 4 5 under I vi IV V. A bass on the roots is the same numbers as the chords' `.add()`, an octave down. That is a whole bass line.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
With chord names there is no `.add()` to copy. Under C, Strudel's voicing puts E at the bottom; a bass on the roots puts C back under it.

<Editor exercise="roots-from-the-names" />

<OnPass>`.rootNotes(2)` reads C, G, Am and F and plays C, G, A and F in octave 2. The same floor as the numbers, read from the names.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Singing the bass is singing the chords' roots, so it is a quick way to hear a loop's shape.

<Sing exercise="sing-the-floor" />

<OnPass>Do, la, fa, sol. Sung, the roots tell you the loop before you have named a single chord.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
The chords are written, and the bass sits on C under all of them. Your loop plays the opening of your song and again its ending, the four bars before the last chord, where Oak's tune and held chords play on top. Once the roots are in, the rhythm is yours: a note a bar, a note a beat, or something that talks to the kick.
