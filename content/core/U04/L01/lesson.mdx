<Step id="S1">
You have written a `vox` line before: the same notes as `mel`, an octave higher. This `vox` sits much closer, and it does not play the same notes.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>`vox` rises when `mel` rises and falls when it falls, always two degrees above. It adds no new tune. It makes the same tune fuller and sweeter, the sound of two people singing in harmony.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Musicians measure the distance between two notes by counting scale notes. Guess before you hear it.

<Predict exercise="count-both-ends" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Home is always the first note here. Listen to how the second note changes the sound: close and warm, then open, then the same note again, higher.

<Example id="three-pairs" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>The distance between two notes is an **interval**, and its count is its name. 1 up to 3 is a 3rd, 1 up to 5 a 5th, and 1 up to the 1 above is an octave. In code the difference is one less than the name, because `n` counts from 0, so degree 5 is `n` 4.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
The hook's `vox` stayed a 3rd above `mel` all the way through. Give this riff the same companion.

<Editor exercise="a-third-above" />

<OnPass>Every note of `vox` now sits a 3rd above `mel`, like the hook. Singing a tune in 3rds is one of the most common ways to harmonise it, in folk duets and pop backing vocals alike.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Now count by ear, with no labels to read.

<EarDrill family="fam.int.in-key" level={1} trials={6} />

<Callout kind="tip">Sing up from do to the second note. The number of syllables you sing is the name of the distance.</Callout>

<OnPass>From home, the degree you hear is already the distance's name. When a pair starts somewhere else, counting takes over.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
A tune used to be one line. `mel` starts as the first two bars of Oak's tune for the second and fourth parts of your song, which Oak plays alone in the second part and with a voice a 3rd above in the fourth. Your two lines take over the tune and its voice in both parts, two bars at a time, and `.gain()` can keep `vox` a little quieter than `mel`.
