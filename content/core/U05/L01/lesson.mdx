<Step id="S1">
In Together Now one line played two notes at once. This loop plays three, in the space between the bass and the tune.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>Each bar stacks three notes over the bass note, and each stack has its own colour: the one on A, in bar 3, sounds darker than the rest. These four chords, on C, G, A and F, carry a great many pop songs, and you will meet them again and again.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
In Together Now a comma put two notes inside one pair of brackets. Nothing stops you from adding a third. <CodeRef fn="," />

<Predict exercise="which-degrees" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Here is the hook's chord part on its own, written as stacks of `n`.

<Example id="the-loop" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>Every stack skips a degree, then another: three notes a 3rd apart. Three notes stacked in 3rds make a **triad**, and you can stack one on any degree.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
Bar 3 is the darker bar from the hook, and its stack has lost two notes.

<Editor exercise="build-bar-three" />

<OnPass>`[5,7,9]` is A, C and E. Each triad here is built on the note the bass plays, and that bottom note names it: its **root**. The bass plays the roots, and the stack above says what kind of chord sits on each.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
The same stacking works with your hands, and no numbers to add.

<OnPass>F, A, C: skip a key, skip a key. The triad on degree 4, with F as its root.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
On the staff a triad looks like a snowman: three notes on three lines in a row, or in three spaces in a row. Skipping a letter each time is what a 3rd looks like on paper, and the numbers under the notes are the degrees you stacked.

<Staff example="on-the-page" />
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
Not every three notes at once make a triad.

<Choice exercise="stack-or-cluster" />

<OnPass>Every pair inside the stack is a 3rd or a 5th, the consonances of Together Now, so it blends and rests. The cluster is built from 2nds: three neighbours fighting for the same space.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S8">
A chord used to be something the band played for you. Now you can build one on any degree. Your four bars play twice through the second part of your song, the eight bars after the opening, over Oak's bass and under Oak's tune. `~` and `@` can give the chords a rhythm, and a tune of your own in degrees, as in Tunes by Number, takes the place of Oak's.
