<Step id="S1">
Four chords, C F G C, over a bass that plays their roots. You will hear them twice: once as you have stacked chords so far, then played the way a pianist's hand would.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>Loop 1 lurches: every chord jumps to a new place. Loop 2 flows, though its chords and its bass are the same. Only where the notes sit has changed.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Now put your ear on one line of notes: the top of each chord.

<Choice exercise="one-hand" />

<OnPass>In card A the chords move like one hand that hardly leaves its place. In card B the hand jumps to a new spot for every chord.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Here is the first change of the gliding version on its own. The note lists say exactly where each note sits.

<Example id="c-to-f" views="code,roll,staff" />

<OnPass>C sits in both chords, so it stays put, and E and G each step up to their neighbour: a half step to F, a whole step to A. A note two neighbouring chords share is a **common tone**.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
Now the change from C to G, the chord on degree 5.

<Editor exercise="c-to-g" />

<OnPass>C E G to B D G: G holds, E falls a whole step to D, C a half step to B. Each line of notes, bottom, middle and top, is a **voice**, and the way each voice moves from one chord to the next is **voice leading**. Here it is smooth: the shared note holds, and the others move to the nearest notes that complete the new chord.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
The bass can leap; the three notes above it should not. F and G share no note, so there every voice has to move, and one of them may need a 3rd. This is the check Oak uses before it hands chord names over to Strudel, and you can try it as often as you like.

<Editor exercise="hand-voiced-cadence" />

<OnPass>Three voices and no leaps: C holds into F, every voice moves a short way into G, and G holds into the last C. That is how a keyboard player's hand moves through these chords, and how choir parts are written, so that each singer has a smooth line to sing.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Back to C G Am F, every chord stacked from its root, so every change leaps. Three of its four changes share a note, so a hand can hold that note and barely move. A chord is no longer three notes for you, but three voices, each with its own short path. Your loop takes over the chords of the first loud part of your song, under Oak's tune and over the bass.
