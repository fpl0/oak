<Step id="S1">
Last lesson fi leaned a tune into sol. Today fi gets a chord of its own. You know the moment from records: the line before a chorus, where the harmony tilts forward and the next chord lands harder than it should.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>Bar 3 tipped forward, and G7 in bar 4 arrived with more weight than before, though only one note had changed. A raised note pulls up toward the note a half step above it, and the chord that holds it pulls with it.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Bar 3 started out as the key's own D minor. One note turns it into D major.

<Predict exercise="which-note-moved" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Two pairs of chords, each stacked by hand from its root. The first pair is the strongest move a key has.

<Example id="two-pairs" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>D7 does to G what G7 does to C: it is G's own V7. G is V in this key, so D7 is the V7 of V. A dominant seventh aimed at a chord other than home is a **secondary dominant**, written V7/V and said "five-seven of five". Three of its notes are the key's own. The fourth, F♯, is fi: G's leading tone, on loan.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
Now the lean with nothing to read, in two keys.

<Choice exercise="find-the-lean" />

<OnPass>Bar 3 in the first loop, bar 2 in the second: each time the bar right before V. That is where a V7 of V lives, and why V sounds so sure of itself afterwards.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
The same lean, now in F major. The target plays it first, so you can hear what bar 3 should do.

<Editor exercise="lean-into-c" />

<OnPass>`G7`: G, B, D and F. The key's own chord on G was minor, with B♭. Raised to B, that note becomes C's leading tone, and bar 3 pulls into bar 4 the way C7 pulls home to F.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
A secondary dominant has two places worth knowing: the note it is built on, and the chord it is aimed at. Its numeral names the second.

<OnPass>IV, V7/V, V7, I. D7 stands on degree 2, but the key's own chord there is D minor. The numeral says what the chord does instead: it is the V7 of V, and the slash reads "of".</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
Inside the three chords one line climbs by the smallest steps there are. Sing it and the lean is in your own throat.

<Sing exercise="sing-the-note-that-moves" />

<OnPass>Fa, fi, sol. Fa belongs to D minor, fi turns the chord into D7, and sol is where fi was always going.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S8">
Your verse reaches V in bar 8 without much conviction. V's own V7, one bar before it, gives the arrival its weight.

<Make exercise="lean-into-the-last-bar" />

<Callout kind="tip">The V of V does not need a whole bar. As in Harmonic Rhythm, `[D7 G7]` puts both chords in one bar, two beats each, and the lean gets quicker.</Callout>

<CanDo id="cd.write.secondary-dominant" />

You can now aim a chord at V: build V's own V7, hear the bar where it leans, and name it V7/V. Next: the same lean aimed at three other chords of the key, inside a soul ballad.

</Step>
