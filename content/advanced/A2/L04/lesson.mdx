<Step id="S1">
Until now every drum you wrote was one hit: a kick, a snare, a hat, placed step by step. This is a whole bar of a drummer in one recording: kick, snare, hats and the quiet notes between them.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>Still one drummer: the same kit and the same touch, the quiet notes carried along with their beats. Only the order of the beats moved. Hip-hop and jungle producers have built whole tracks this way, from one short recorded drum break.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
The recording is one bar long, and the loop plays at its tempo, 96 beats a minute, so each quarter of it is one beat of the loop.

<Predict exercise="last-piece-first" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Each piece carries everything the drummer played in that beat. Change the order of the numbers and the beats move with all they hold.

<Example id="look-inside" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>Beat 2 holds the first loud snare, and putting piece 0 on beat 3 brings the kick of beat 1 back there. A recording played back as a sound is a **sample**: a whole bar like this one, or a single hit. Every `bd` you have written was one. Oak made this break itself from public-domain drum hits, so it is free to use in anything you make.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
Bar 3 of the opening's loop 2 swapped two beats. Two numbers decide which piece plays on beats 2 and 3.

<Editor exercise="swap-the-middle" />

<OnPass>The same drummer, a new groove: the kick comes a beat early, the snare a beat late, and nobody had to play it again. Cutting a sample into equal pieces and playing them in a new order is called a **chop**. `.splice()` does the same and also fits each piece's speed to its place. `.loopAt()` fits a whole break to another tempo, and `.begin()` and `.end()` pick any stretch of a sample by hand.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
The drums alone this time, so every beat is plain. Count along.

<Choice exercise="which-order" />

<OnPass>The two middle beats swapped. You found it by the loud hits: the ear hangs a groove on its kick and snare, so moving one piece changes the whole feel.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
Oak's pack has six more breaks, each two bars long and each recorded at its own tempo: `brkfunk` at 100 beats a minute, `brkboombap` at 90, `brkjungle` at 170, `brkhouse` at 124, `brkjazz` at 140 and `brklofi` at 80. Your song runs at 90.

<Example id="three-fits" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>`brkboombap` was recorded at 90, so each piece fills its beat. The jungle break was recorded at 170: each of its pieces ends just past halfway through our beat, and a gap follows. `.splice()` stretches every piece to its beat by playing it slower, and slower is lower: half the speed is an octave down, twice the speed an octave up, the doubling of Waves & Harmonics. To keep a break's own sound, pick one recorded at your tempo.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S8">
On the old samplers there was no way to change a recording's speed without changing its pitch and its length too: one dial did all three. Producers turned that into a rhythm machine. <CodeRef fn="speed" />

<Editor exercise="speed-changes-the-rhythm" />

<OnPass>Twice the speed, and the same drummer plays double time, an octave up; half the speed, half time, an octave down. You changed the speed, and the rhythm came with it: the pieces got shorter or longer, and the bar held more of them or fewer. A tempo knob that also bends pitch is a whole instrument of its own.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Your drum parts used to be hits placed one at a time. Now a recorded break is material to cut and rearrange, and this one drives the break section of your song, over Oak's bass and chords. Producers also cut found sounds in between the drums: the pack has `snap`, `swoosh`, `impact`, `scratch`, `drone`, and `foley`, eleven recorded knocks and slaps picked with `.n()`. Sixteen pieces give you smaller steps to move.
