<Step id="S1">
You can comp a ii–V–I in shells. Many jazz tunes change key every few bars and use a ii–V–I to get from one key to the next, so a player needs it everywhere. Here your trio, with a lick on the vibes, sets off.

<Example id="tour-four" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>Home moves every time, always to the key a 4th above the last: C, F, B♭, E♭. The shells and the lick travel with it, so each key sounds like the same phrase in a new place.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
The whole tour, all twelve keys. Every number in `tour` is the one before it plus five. When that would climb past 8, take 12 off: the trio stays in a playable range, and the key is the same.

<Example id="tour-twelve" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>Each key is a 4th above the last: C, F, B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭, G♭, B, E, A, D, G, and then C again. That order is the **cycle of fourths**, the circle of fifths walked the other way. Jazz harmony moves this way all the time: inside a ii–V–I, each root is a 4th above the one before.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
The tour so far visits two keys.

<Editor exercise="one-key-further" />

<OnPass>Three keys, one shape: the shells, the bass and the lick all moved together.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
A chart cannot say "transpose": it names every chord. Here is the same tour, written out the way a jazz player reads it.

<Editor exercise="a-flat-by-name" />

<OnPass>B♭m7, E♭7, A♭maj7: every chord a 4th above the same chord in E♭. Moving and naming are the same skill.</OnPass>
</Step>
