<Step id="S1">
You can play a ii–V–I in any key and hear it land. Jazz players also swap its chords for others that land just as well. Here are the last four bars of a ballad, twice; only bar 2 changes.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>In the first take the bass leaps down a 5th onto home. In the second it slides down in small steps instead: bar 2 takes on a darker colour, and the ending lands just as surely. The vibes play the same notes over both.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Both endings in one loop. How small were the steps?

<Predict exercise="where-the-bass-goes" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Why does the second ending still land? Strip both chords down to their 3rd and 7th and look.

<Example id="shared" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>The same two notes, F and B. In G7 they are the 7th and the 3rd; in D♭7 they are the 3rd and the 7th, with B spelled C♭. The two roots sit a tritone apart, so the chords swap their guide tones and keep the same pull.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
The same move in F. One chord changes, and the bass follows it, because both lines read the same string.

<Editor exercise="slide-into-f" />

<OnPass>Gm7, G♭7, Fmaj7: the bass walks G, G♭, F, and the chord in bar 2 still leans into F. A dominant replaced by the dominant a tritone away is a **tritone substitution**, written ♭II7 for V7.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Now in your head first: the substitute in E♭, without the editor.

<Choice exercise="substitute-in-e-flat" />

<OnPass>E7, or F♭7 if you spell it from E♭: six half steps from B♭, and its bass slides down a half step onto E♭.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Two ways home, by ear. Listen to how the bass gets to the last chord.

<EarDrill family="fam.jzz.subs" level={1} trials={4} />

<OnPass>A fall of a 5th, or a slide of a half step. This drill comes back in your warm-ups; later it adds a third way home.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
Every dominant you write now has a twin a tritone away, and you choose which one the bass walks through. This turnaround opens your song: your four bars play twice under Oak's top line, then the tune starts in G. That line holds notes each dominant shares with its twin; a vibes line of your own would take its place.

<Make exercise="your-turnaround" />

</Step>
