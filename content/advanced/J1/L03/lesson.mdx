<Step id="S1">
The chart you read last lesson closed on a C7 that leaned into F. Here that lean grows into a longer approach. The trio plays four bars that come to rest, then the same four bars with a different home.

<Step id="S3">
The pull you heard has a shape you can read, in the numerals and in the bass.

<Example id="the-gesture" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>ii7, V7, Imaj7: the minor seventh on 2, the dominant seventh on 5, home on the major seventh on 1. The bass falls a 5th from each root to the next (or climbs a 4th, the same letter), and each chord pulls into the one after it. Heard as one gesture, it is the **ii–V–I**, the most common sentence in jazz harmony.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
The same gesture in B♭ major. The letters change; the numerals do not.

<Choice exercise="number-it-in-b-flat" />

<OnPass>ii7, V7, Imaj7 in B♭: Cm7, F7, B♭maj7. Because the numerals hold in every key, jazz players learn the ii–V–I as one shape and carry it anywhere.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
The second take from the start of the lesson moved this gesture to a new home.

<Editor exercise="home-on-f" />

<OnPass>Gm7, C7, Fmaj7: the ii–V–I with F as home, the second trio you heard at the start.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
No chord band this time: phrases in many keys, and endings that only sound close.

<Choice exercise="does-it-land" />

<OnPass>The ii–V–I by ear, against the two endings that sound most like it.</OnPass>
</Step>
