<Step id="S1">
Last lesson one bass figure made a groove lean. Here a rimshot pattern two bars long sits under a son groove in C with a piano tune, then the same groove with one line changed.

<Example id="son-groove" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>In the first loop every note of the tune lands with a rimshot, and the groove sits. In the second the rimshot's two bars have traded places, and not one tune note meets it: the tune and the rhythm under it pull apart.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
The rimshot's two bars are not the same. Let your taps find the difference.

<Tap exercise="tap-the-rimshot" />

<OnPass>Three taps, then two. The first bar is the tresillo you wrote last time; the second answers it on beats 2 and 3.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Alone over the hats, the rimshot shows what each of its two bars holds.

<Example id="the-clave" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>Five hits over two bars: a bar of three, the tresillo, and a bar of two. That two-bar rhythm is the **clave**, the key the whole groove locks to. Its bar of three is the three-side, its bar of two the two-side, and which one comes first is its direction: this one runs 3-2.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
The tune decides which way the clave runs, not the other way round.

<Editor exercise="turn-it-round" />

<OnPass>The clave now runs 2-3, and the tune's two opening notes land on its two-side. Same five hits, other order, and the groove sits.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
There is a second clave, a close relative of the son's. Listen for the one hit that moves.

<Choice exercise="one-hit-moves" />

<OnPass>The third hit of the three-side lands one eighth later, on the last eighth of the bar. That is the rumba clave: the same two-side, a three-side that leans harder into the next bar.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
The bass plays C in the first bar of each groove, so you always know which bar is which.

<Choice exercise="which-side-first" />

<OnPass>Three hits in the first bar is 3-2, two hits in the first bar is 2-3. The hits never change; only the order does, and the order is what a tune has to agree with.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
You can count the clave, name its direction, and turn it to face a tune. The rhythm takes its name from the instrument that plays it in Cuba: the claves, two hardwood sticks struck together. In Strudel they are `clave`.

<Example id="on-the-claves" views="code,roll" />

Your tune and your clave become the chorus of your song, after its first verse, in place of Oak's. Build the tune from chord tones, C's in bar 1 and G7's in bar 2, and land them on the clave's hits.

<Make exercise="your-tune-on-the-clave" />

<Recap />
</Step>
