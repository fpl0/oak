<Step id="S1">
The son clave spread five hits over two bars. This bossa nova groove packs five rimshots into one, over a kick, an upright bass and a Rhodes.

<Example id="bossa" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>Both loops play five rimshots a bar, but only the first sways. In the second the longest gap has moved to the end of the bar, and the rhythm ticks along without its lilt.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Five hits a bar, and the gaps between them are not all the same. Let your taps measure them.

<Tap exercise="tap-the-rim" />

<OnPass>Five taps a bar: three sixteenths apart, three more, then a gap of four after the third hit, then three and three to the bar line. The long gap in the middle is what makes it lilt.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
`(3,8)` was the tresillo. The two numbers work with any pair: hits first, steps second.

<Example id="three-lines" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>Four hits over sixteen steps is a plain count of four. Three over eight is the tresillo, and five over sixteen is five hits three steps apart with one gap of four. Hits spread as evenly as the steps allow make a **Euclidean rhythm**, and the most even spacing is not always a plain one.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Back to the bossa. Its rimshot is `(5,16)` started from another step.

<Editor exercise="find-the-bossa" />

<OnPass>The same five hits and the same gaps, started from another step, so the long gap now falls after the third hit and a hit stays on beat 1. Starting a repeating rhythm from a different step is a **rotation**, and this one is the bossa nova rim.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
The hats are your ruler. Count the hits, decide the steps, then listen for where the rhythm starts.

<Choice exercise="name-the-numbers" />

<OnPass>Rhythm 1 is three hits over sixteen steps, five, five and six sixteenths apart: its longest gap comes last, where the tresillo's shortest does. Rhythm 2 is the cinquillo started one step later, so the bar opens on a rest.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
The method form says the same thing, and its numbers can change from bar to bar.

<Editor exercise="numbers-that-change" />

<OnPass>One number patterned: three hits in bar 1, five in bar 2, on the same eight steps. Every number of `.euclid()` and `.euclidRot()` can move this way.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S8">
Two numbers now write a whole family of rhythms, and a third turns any of them round: many traditional bell and drum patterns, from West Africa to Cuba and Brazil, turn out to be Euclidean rhythms or rotations of them. In your song, the eight bars after the second verse are a break for the bells, where Oak's cowbell plays `(5,16)` over C and `(5,16,8)` over G7, and your lines take over its percussion. A tresillo kick from two lessons ago sits well under a five-hit bell.
