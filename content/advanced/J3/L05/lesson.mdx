<Step id="S1">
Your rootless voicings left the root to the bass and built each chord from its 3rd, 5th, 7th and 9th. Pianists also stack chords from one interval alone, and then the bass has even more to say. The same four Rhodes notes, over two bass notes.

<Example id="over-d" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>Over D the four notes make Dm11: dark and settled, a minor chord with its 11th. Over G the same four make G7sus, a chord that hangs and waits to move. The Rhodes never changed. The bass gave the chord its name.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Held over the bass, the stack stands still on the roll.

<Example id="held-stack" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>Every gap is 5 half steps, a 4th: D G C F. The lowest of the four is the bass's own note, an octave up.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Now build stacks yourself, one on each given note.

<Editor exercise="stack-the-fourths" />

<OnPass>Every chord is a stack of 4ths. A chord built this way is a **quartal voicing**: open and hollow, no two neighbours a 3rd apart, so it waits for the bass to tell it what it is. Over D and E, these are Dm11 and Em11.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
The stack on D can be more than one chord. Here the bass makes it hang.

<Editor exercise="the-bass-names-it" />

<OnPass>D under D G C F gives the stack its root, and the four notes become Dm11's root, 11th, 7th and minor 3rd. The bass did all of it: the Rhodes played the same notes as before.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
A chord's name is also its job: where a tune can rest, or where it has to keep going.

<OnPass>After A7 the ear expects a chord on D. Over D the stack is Dm11 and lands home; over G it is G7sus and still leans somewhere else.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Your stacks of 4ths, your bass, over the Band's drums: the sound of a jazz pianist comping on one minor chord for bars at a time. Bring back the comping rhythm of your rootless loop, or hold the chords. Your song's rooms move through ii–V–I and this loop stays on minor 11th chords, so it stays a sketch of its own.
