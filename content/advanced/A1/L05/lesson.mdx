<Step id="S1">
Space left your loop placed: a plucked bass, a riff that echoes, a snare in a room. Every tool so far has kept it clean. This lesson does some damage on purpose.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>For most ears the bass sounds bigger in loop 2: it growls and carries. The hats turn grainy, like an old record. Loop 2's bass is turned down to match loop 1's level, so what you heard is the roughness itself, not extra volume.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Push a sound harder than it can go and it changes character. Here the bass is pushed a little harder each bar. <CodeRef fn="distort" />

<Predict exercise="push-the-bass" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
The scope shows what pushing does to the shape of a wave. A sine is the plainest shape there is.

<Example id="flat-tops" views="code" />

<OnPass>At 3 the round hill is squashed flat, close to a square, and it buzzes like one. A new shape is a new set of harmonics. Pushing a sound until its peaks round off and flatten is **saturation**. A little makes a part warm and thick, a lot makes it harsh. Either way it gets louder, so turn a saturated part down before you judge it.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
A growl, not a rasp. Louder nearly always sounds better at first hearing, so compare the target and yours at the same level.

<Editor exercise="warm-the-bass" />

<OnPass>A bass with a growl under the riff, still in proportion. On small speakers that growl carries the bass line further, because the new harmonics sit where small speakers can play them.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
The other way to damage a sound is to make it coarser. Early digital samplers stored sound in 8 or 12 bits, and their grain became a sound producers now add on purpose. <CodeRef fn="crush" />

<Choice exercise="fewer-bits" />

<OnPass>Sixteen bits is too fine to hear. At four, every point of the wave snaps to one of sixteen steps, and a hard grain rides on each note. An effect that rebuilds a sound from fewer steps of level is a **bitcrusher**; each bit fewer halves the steps. `.coarse()` does the same to time: it holds each point of the wave a little longer.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Two kinds of grit, and one change that is not grit at all. Listen to the riff the second time round.

<Choice exercise="which-grit" />

<OnPass>Crushed: a hard grain rode on top of each riff note, and on its echoes too, since they repeat the crushed sound. Saturation would have thickened the note itself and pushed it forward.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
In your song, this loop opens and closes the song, where Oak plays the band as an old record: its chords low-passed and crushed to six bits, over a crackle bed. That lo-fi colour is three tools together: saturation, fewer bits and a bed of noise. Here `.drive()` pushes the ladder filter from Filters harder, so it adds grit, the hats lose bits, and `s("crackle")` clicks under it all, fewer clicks the lower `.density()` is. `vinyl` and `tape` are recorded beds of record noise and tape hiss. <CodeRef fn="drive" />

<Example id="old-record" views="code" />

The checklist requires `.distort()`, `.crush()` or `.coarse()`; `.drive()` and a bed of noise are on it as extras. Choose where the dirt goes and how much, by ear, and keep every part where it sat before. Grit on the riff rides into its echoes from Space, so a little goes a long way there.
