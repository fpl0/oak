<Step id="S1">
In Tones & Timbres a sine sounded pure and a sawtooth buzzed. This lesson finds out why, starting from a loop like the ones you write: kick, snare, hats, a bass line and a two-bar riff.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>In loop 2 the bass steps forward and the riff gains an edge, and no note changed. A sine is the plainest wave there is. The others carry more inside each note, and the rest of this lesson looks inside.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Now the four waves one at a time, on a single note. Bright is the edge you just heard on the riff.

<Predict exercise="which-is-brightest" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
A sound is air moving back and forth, many times a second. The scope draws that movement: time runs left to right, the air pushes up and pulls down. <CodeRef fn="scope" />

<Example id="see-the-wave" views="code" />

<OnPass>Sine: a round curve. Triangle: straight lines up and down. Square: flat at the top, flat at the bottom. Sawtooth: a slow climb and a sudden drop. Each wave is named after its shape, and each shape has its own sound.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
Bar 2 hides something about bar 1. Listen to the sawtooth's single note, then to the six notes after it.

<Example id="notes-inside" views="code" />

<OnPass>How many times a wave repeats each second is its **frequency**, counted in hertz (Hz): a2 is 110, a3 220, a4 440. Bar 2 climbs by about 110 each time, on the nearest notes of the keyboard. All six sound inside the sawtooth's a2. You hear one note, with quieter, higher notes stacked on it.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
`.partials()` takes a list, one number per note in that stack, from the bottom. On a sawtooth, a square or a triangle, 1 keeps that note at the wave's own level, 0 leaves it out, and anything between turns it down. On a sine it does nothing, because a sine has only the bottom one. <CodeRef fn="partials" />

<Editor exercise="build-a-sawtooth" />

<OnPass>Four on, and the riff has its edge back. The stack of notes at 1, 2, 3, 4 … times a note's frequency is its **harmonic series**, and each one is a harmonic. Which harmonics a sound has, and how loud each one is, is a large part of its timbre.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Each wave is a recipe of harmonics, and the ear can learn to tell the recipes apart.

<Choice exercise="odd-ones-only" />

<OnPass>The square: harmonics 1, 3, 5 and up, none of the even ones. That gap is what makes it hollow, where the sawtooth, with all of them, buzzes.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
At the start of this lesson a wave was a name in `.s()`. Now you can open it and choose what it keeps. A bass with harmonics carries on headphones and small speakers alike, because the ear hears its low note through the harmonics above it.
