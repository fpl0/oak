<Step id="S1">
Waves & Harmonics started from four plain waves. This last sound lesson builds richer sources out of them. Here is the loop as Space left it, with a quiet pad under the riff.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>A struck bell: each riff note in loop 2 clangs with a metallic ring, yet it is still one sine wave underneath. Behind it the pad spreads wide. Both sounds are built from waves you already know, and the next steps take each one apart.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
In Movement a slow wave moved a note's pitch a few times a second, and you heard a wobble. Make that second wave fast, hundreds of times a second, and the wobble is gone. <CodeRef fn="fm" />

<Predict exercise="bend-it-further" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
The same bend in bars 2 and 3. Only the speed of the second sine changes.

<Example id="three-ratios" views="code" />

<OnPass>Bar 1 is a plain sine. In bar 2 the second sine runs at twice the note, so its new tones land on the note's harmonics: one clear pitch, hollow and reedy. In bar 3 it runs at 3.5 times the note, so some new tones fall between the harmonics, and the note clangs like a bell. Letting one wave bend another's pitch this fast is **FM synthesis**. `.fm()` sets how far, `.fmh()` how fast.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
This is the riff from the opening's loop 2. Rebuild its bell from the plain sine.

<Editor exercise="ring-the-riff" />

<OnPass>A deep bend, at a speed between two whole numbers: a struck bell from one sine wave. Bend less for something softer, or use a whole number for a brighter, brassier tone. FM works on the synth waves only; a recorded instrument such as the piano has no oscillator to bend. FM synthesizers gave many 1980s records their bright electric pianos and bells.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
The other rich source of this lesson is the pad from the first step: a supersaw, five sawtooth waves for every note.

<Choice exercise="wide-and-narrow" />

<OnPass>Card B. Pulled apart, the five saws drift in and out of step, so the chord shimmers and spreads; at one pitch they add up to a single still wave. Tuning copies of a sound a fraction of a half step apart is **detune**. `.detune()` sets how far apart, `.unison()` how many copies.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Three sources, one riff, and no code to read.

<Choice exercise="which-source" />

<OnPass>FM: the tones between the harmonics give it away. You heard one pitch, with a metallic edge that fades as the note dies.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
For a longer session: the pad behind your bell riff, made wider by ear. <CodeRef fn="unison" />

<Editor exercise="thicken-the-pad" />

<OnPass>More copies, pulled further apart: a pad that fills the space behind the riff. Too much detune and the chord starts to sound out of tune; stop just before that. The pulse wave, `s("pulse")`, is another source to try, its tone set with `.pw()`.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S8">
In your song, this loop plays the last chorus, the loudest part, with Oak's chords still under your pad; in the electronic style Oak's tune there is already a bell made with FM, over a supersaw. Pick a part of the plain loop, or of a loop you bring, and give it a new sound: bend it a little for brightness, a lot for a clang, and choose the speed by ear. FM needs a synth wave to bend. The riff's quick fade is the pluck from Envelopes: a bell needs that struck shape as much as its clang.
