<Step id="S1">
Your walking bass leans into every bar line. A tune's last bars lean on a larger scale: their job is to send the tune back to the top. The trio plays that turn in E♭, first the way doo-wop and jazz both know it, then with two chords swapped.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>In the first take the second chord sits back, a calm minor chord. In the second it leans hard into the chord after it, and the whole turn pulls more urgently toward home.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
What makes that chord lean? Here are both takes back to back, as one eight-bar loop.

<Example id="hook-again" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>C7 is the V7 of Fm7: its root falls a 5th into the ii, the way V7 falls into I. Gm7 keeps three of E♭maj7's four notes, so it can stand in for I. Together they turn I–vi–ii–V into iii–VI–ii–V, and the dominant on 6 makes the turn pull harder on its way home.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Now by numeral. The chord band is off; the four chords are named in the prompt.

<OnPass>iii7, V7/ii, ii7, V7. Jazz players often call the second chord VI7, by the degree it stands on; V7/ii says where it leads, and that is the name Oak's numerals use.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
One string drives the comping and the bass, so a change to `changes` moves both lines.

<Editor exercise="brighten-the-turn" />

<OnPass>Gm7, C7, Fm7, B♭7: each root a 5th below the one before, G, C, F, B♭, a chain that pulls all the way back to the top.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
By ear, in two keys. Both turnarounds end on ii and V; the difference is at the start.

<OnPass>The tell is the second chord: a minor chord sits back, a dominant leans into the ii. Both turns close the last bars of many standards.</OnPass>
</Step>
