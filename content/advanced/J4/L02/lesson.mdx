<Step id="S1">
The twelve-bar blues was one of the first forms you learned by heart. Jazz players keep its frame and fill it with the ii–Vs and turnarounds you now know. The trio plays the form twice: first the plain blues, then the jazz version, with a muted trumpet on the tune.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>Where the plain blues sits on one chord for bars at a time, the second take keeps moving: a lift into bar 5, a climb through bar 6, and a chain of chords from bar 8 back to the top. The frame and the landings are the same; the road between them is busier.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Name what moved: both charts play back to back.

<Example id="both-charts" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>Bar 4 leads into bar 5 with a ii–V, Cm7 F7 into B♭7. Bar 6 climbs back to F7 through B°7. Bars 8 to 10 are a chain into the last line, D7 to Gm7 to C7, and bar 12 turns around to the top. Seven bars differ, and the form still lands where the blues lands. That is a **jazz blues**, one common chart among several: some players put Am7 D7 in bar 8, or stay on F7 in bar 2.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Now write the chart down, the way a jazz player would read it.

<OnPass>F7 B♭7 F7, Cm7 F7, B♭7 B°7 F7 D7, Gm7 C7, F7 D7, Gm7 C7. Twelve bars you can now read at a glance.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
The comping and the bass both read `changes`.

<Editor exercise="put-the-jazz-chords-in" />

<OnPass>The jazz blues from one string: the bass follows every chord, two to the bar where the chart has two. In bar 6 the Rhodes keeps B♭7's D and A♭, the guide tones B°7 shares with it, while the bass climbs a half step from B♭ to B. One note moves, and the chord tightens before F7.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Twelve bars you knew by heart now carry the harmony jazz players hear in them. The trio starts on the plain blues with nothing on top yet; the muted trumpet is yours.

</Step>
